When Every Trader Uses the Same AI: A Simulation of Market Fragility

If a crowd of AI traders reads the same public data through similar models, their decisions converge — and that convergence can become a systemic stampede. A synthetic + LLM-agent simulation of when private rationality turns into shared fragility, and one hedge that measurably helps.

CK
Chen Kai June 10, 2026 · 10 min read · 982 words

阅读中文版 →

Here is a question that keeps me up at night, and it is not about any single model being wrong. It is about what happens when everyone is right in the same way.

Suppose a large fraction of market participants stop reading the tape themselves and delegate the read to an AI. Suppose, further, that many of them use similar models — the same few frontier LLMs, fine-tuned on overlapping data, prompted in overlapping ways. Each agent is individually sharper than the human it replaced. Does the market get more efficient, or does it quietly load up on a new kind of risk?

I built a simulation to find out. It is a mechanism-and-design study on synthetic data — not investment advice, and not evidence about any real market. But the mechanism it isolates is, I think, real.

The setup: signal vs decision, one shared market#

Every period, each simulated trader receives the same public, noisy market packet. From it they must produce two things, deliberately separated:

  • a SIGNAL — a cheap up/down prediction (a fast, System-1 read)
  • a DECISION — a deliberate capital order, sized by risk appetite (a slow, System-2 commitment)

This split matters. The danger I am hunting is not that AIs predict badly. It is that they predict the same way, and then act on it at the same time.

I run two engines on the identical market. One is a deterministic synthetic control — numbers all the way down, so the mechanism is legible and reproducible. The other swaps in real LLM agents drawing on several different model families, so I can dial the “how similar are everyone’s models” knob for real. Then I vary two things: how many participants use AI at all (adoption), and how correlated their models are (commonality).

What the numbers say#

Six canonical scenarios, from a human-only baseline to a high-adoption monoculture where almost everyone uses almost the same model. Twenty-four agents, twelve assets, a hundred-plus trading periods, averaged across repeated runs. The trend is monotonic and, honestly, a little chilling.

Human-only0.24—1.8%

Read the columns from the top down. As adoption and model-commonality rise:

  • Beliefs converge. Portfolio convergence more than doubles, from 0.24 to 0.57 — the agents increasingly hold the same positions.
  • Errors correlate. This is the one that matters most. Under common models, the agents’ forecast errors are about three times as correlated as under diverse models (0.22 vs 0.08). Their individual accuracy is roughly the same. The danger is not that they are worse — it is that they are wrong together.
  • Stampede risk explodes. A crowded-trade alarm — which fires only when concentration, consensus, and leverage line up at once — goes off 1.8% of the time in the human baseline and 41.8% of the time in the monoculture. More than a twentyfold increase in the frequency of the conditions that precede a forced, correlated unwind.

The private-vs-social wedge#

Here is the part that makes this a genuinely hard problem rather than a simple “AI bad” story. As the market moves toward monoculture, individual welfare improves. The AI really is more accurate than the human it replaced; each agent, on its own terms, is making a better decision. Every participant is behaving rationally, and every participant is a little better off.

And yet crowding, tail risk, and the stampede-alarm rate all get worse at the same time. Privately rational, socially fragile. Nobody in the simulation is making a mistake. The fragility is an emergent property of everyone making the same correct-looking call and acting on it in sync. That gap — between what is good for each agent and what is safe for the system — is the whole thesis, and it is visible directly in the run.

The hedge that actually helps#

The useful thing about a simulation is that you can fork it. Take a run at the moment the crowd is converging, change exactly one knob — inject diversity, add noise to a fraction of the agents, force some of them to disagree — and let it play forward as a clean counterfactual on the same market.

It works, measurably. Injecting diversity drops portfolio convergence from about 0.35 back toward 0.27. A “consensus hedge” — deliberately holding some capital against the crowded trade — posts the lowest tail loss of any scenario and cuts the alarm rate. The correlation of errors, the thing that makes a monoculture dangerous, falls by roughly half when you break the model-commonality.

None of this requires the AIs to be worse. It requires them to be different from each other. Diversity, here, is not a fairness nicety; it is a load-bearing risk control.

Why I think the mechanism is real#

I want to be careful about the limits. This is synthetic data. The market model is a caricature. The numbers above are properties of my simulation, not forecasts about anything you can trade. If you take one figure from this piece to a trading desk, you have misread it.

But strip away the specifics and the mechanism is uncomfortably general: shared inputs plus shared models produce correlated outputs; correlated outputs sized with leverage produce crowded trades; crowded trades unwind together. That chain does not depend on the details of my toy market. It depends only on sameness — and the entire trajectory of the industry is toward more people using fewer, more similar models.

The reassuring version of the AI-in-finance story is “smarter participants, more efficient markets.” The simulation suggests a second-order term the reassuring version leaves out: the same force that makes each participant smarter also makes them more alike, and alikeness is exactly what a systemic stampede is made of. Worth watching. Worth, at minimum, keeping some diversity in the system on purpose.
